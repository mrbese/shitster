The manifesto

Anger is a signal. Action is the point.

shitster started with something that happened to me. BMW put a movie promotion on the dashboard of a car I own. I could have written a rant. Instead, I made DashOff.

Then I noticed that the feeling did not stop at my car. It shows up whenever somebody uses a contract, a regulation, a safety argument, or plain institutional inertia to make an unfair thing look inevitable.

I do not think a practice becomes fair because the target is big, foreign, unpopular, or capable of fighting back. If it bothers me when it happens to a consumer, it still bothers me when it happens to an outsider, a challenger, or a company.

The shitster budget

Every company should expect one.

A shitster budget is money, time, and stubbornness reserved for pushing back when somebody turns legality into a shield for unfairness.

It can fund research, a small independent venture, a claim, a public record, or a campaign. The form is not the point. The point is refusing to treat a bad rule as the end of the conversation.

The people who write the rules have been using legal arbitrage for years. Reading the rulebook and answering back is not a dirty trick. It is the whole idea.

A recurring test

The two-face test.

I pay attention when a rule is sold as universal but stops at the boundary of somebody else's industry, safety rationale, or commercial interest.

Interoperability can be a public principle for an integrated digital platform, then a safety question for an integrated vehicle system. A charging standard can be environmental necessity in one product category and disappear in another. I am interested in the gap between those explanations.

This is not a list of conclusions pretending to be campaigns. It is a test: if this principle is real, why does it stop here?
